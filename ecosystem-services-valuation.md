# Ecosystem Services Valuation: A Case Study of Offshore Wind Farm Development

---

## Abstract

Ecosystem Services has influenced our view of the relationship between nature and human culture. Preservation of nature was formerly regarded as something for which we need to forfeit our comfort; the environment is now considered natural capital and a nation's vital resource. However, ecosystem services are progressively limited. One way to prevent this is to encourage recognition of the value of natural capital in society.

Valuation of the services obtained from the ecosystem demonstrates its contribution to society and society's high dependency on the ecosystem. It should be noted that ecosystems have infinite value because they support all human activity. However, the more relevant application of Ecosystem Services Valuation in terms of environmental decision-making is to assess marginal changes in the environment.

This report analyzes the monetary benefit that Ecosystem Services Valuation provides. It describes the origin of ecosystem services, the underlying principles, and their development over the last two decades. It also describes the process of Ecosystem Services Valuation and how it has been integrated into environmental decision-making. A hypothetical offshore wind farm case study is discussed to show how Ecosystem Services Valuation is used in practice to influence decision-making, as well as the arguments and criticisms against its use.

## Development of Ecosystem Services Valuation

Ecosystem services are the benefits humans obtain, directly or indirectly, from the ecosystem.¹ These services include goods such as food, water, and biochemicals; regulating services such as flood reduction and water purification; and cultural services, which include cultural diversity and aesthetic values.² Ecosystems are also referred to as natural capital, because they provide these goods and services in a way similar to how goods and services are obtained from human, financial, and manufactured capital.³

The table below shows the classification of ecosystem services by the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment.

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Provisioning Services** | Food, water, fuel, biochemical and genetic resources |
| **Regulating Services** | Climate regulation, disease regulation, water purification, water regulation, and pollination |
| **Cultural Services** (abstract goods) | Spiritual and religious value, recreation and ecotourism, aesthetic value, inspiration, education |
| **Supporting Services** | Soil formation, nutrient cycling, primary production, habitat production, production of atmospheric oxygen |

*Fig 2. Source: Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005)*

A more comprehensive classification of ecosystem services, compiled by Costanza et al. (1997), can be found in their article "The Value of the World's Ecosystem Services and Natural Capital."

The steady rise in population and standards of living has resulted in a surge in demand for ecosystem services. Conversely, ecosystems are undergoing deterioration and, consequently, are increasingly failing to provide these goods and services. A lack of valuation contributes to the shortage of ecosystem services, because society fails to properly utilize the services the ecosystem provides if it is not aware of their value. Society tends to take ecosystem services for granted, regarding their supply as though it will continue indefinitely. As a result, people are often unaware of the benefits ecosystem services provide.⁴ Ecosystem Services Valuation, therefore, is a means of addressing this issue.

The concept of ecosystem services was introduced in the late 1970s and 1980s by authors such as Walter Westman (Westman, 1977) and Ehrlich and Ehrlich (1981), building on earlier literature highlighting the societal value of nature's functions.⁵,⁶

Van Wilgen et al. noted that Walter Westman, in his paper "How Much Are Nature's Services Worth?" (Westman, 1977), raised the issue of valuing the conservation of ecosystems for the services they provide to society, and stated that developed nations have demanded quantification of the value of these services in monetary terms.⁶

The term "environmental services" was first used in 1981 by Ehrlich and Ehrlich (1997), though it had earlier appeared in the report "The Study of Critical Environmental Problems" (SCEP, 1970), written for the first World Summit, held in Stockholm, Sweden, in 1972.⁷ The report identified ecosystem services such as climate regulation, soil retention, pest control, and fisheries. The justification for the ecosystem services concept was largely academic, used mainly by scientists to demonstrate the effects of biodiversity loss on ecosystem functions underpinning human well-being, thereby expressing the need for conservation.⁸

Ehrlich and Mooney (1983) conceptualized ecosystem services to demonstrate the benefits these services provide to humans. The underlying rationale was to draw attention to the fact that, unlike other economic resources, ecological resources may have no substitutes. Building on work begun in the 1970s, this helped establish the idea that the ecosystem provides poorly understood and insufficiently accounted-for value.⁷ These researchers were active in advocating for the conservation of nature, and their work was duly recognized and published.⁹

The concept of ecosystem services was finally mainstreamed between 1997 and 2000. According to S. Chaudhary et al., this was achieved through two major developments: the publication of studies by Costanza et al. (1997) and Daily (1997). The former focused on placing monetary values on ecosystem services, while the latter stressed the importance of the ecosystem to society and the need to connect it with policy.¹⁰

The transition of the ecosystem services concept from academia into policy, as noted by S. Chaudhary et al., was the second major development. A significant milestone occurred when Payment for Ecosystem Services was launched in Costa Rica in 1997, in response to increased global attention to and commodification of ecosystem services. However, it has been noted that the launch was not entirely successful, as it reflected political dynamics around forest degradation rather than a genuine transfer of academic debate into practice.¹⁰

## Principles of Ecosystem Services Valuation

In principle, environmental scientists believe that placing monetary values on ecosystem services will influence how people allocate rights to things and activities, and will help them recognize the benefits obtained from those services. Ecosystem Services Valuation, when integrated into policy, can assist decision-making.¹,³,¹¹,¹²

The approach to valuing ecosystem services is based on changes in people's preferences, which are affected by marginal changes in the quantity or quality of goods and services.¹³ According to E. Gómez-Baggethun et al. (2009), the monetary figures presented in Costanza et al.'s 1997 report have had a high impact on policymaking, increasing the development and use of monetary valuation studies. However, Spangenberg et al. (2010) argue that the underlying principles of Ecosystem Services Valuation are idealistic, and that the value of ecosystem services may vary depending on the valuation method adopted — meaning such valuations may not provide an objective estimate of these services.¹⁴

The nature of some ecosystem services precludes them from having a market price, making them prone to market failure. This is because ecosystem services are non-market goods, so their prices cannot be determined the way other goods' prices are. For this reason, several methods have been developed to assess ecosystem service value. Valuation techniques can be divided into two categories: **revealed preference** and **stated preference** methods. The former relies on existing market data, namely Travel Cost (TC) and Hedonic Pricing (HP). The latter is based on data collected via questionnaires, with Contingent Valuation being one such method.¹⁵ These valuation techniques are described further below:

- **Avoidance Cost (AC):** Services are appraised based on the costs that would have been incurred if the services were not available.
- **Travel Cost (TC):** These services require people to travel to use them — a recreational park, for example. The value placed on such an area should be at least equal to the cost incurred by people traveling to use it.
- **Replacement Cost:** Natural system services could be replaced with artificial systems, and the loss of the service is assessed in terms of the cost of replacing it.
- **Factor Income (FI):** Provision of these services increases returns — for example, improved water quality boosting income for the fishery sector.
- **Conjoint Analysis:** Different service scenarios are presented, and people are asked to choose or rank them.
- **Contingent Valuation:** This method involves asking people whether they are willing to pay, or willing to accept compensation, for a change in an ecological service — usually via survey questionnaires.
- **Benefit Transfer:** This involves transferring existing ESV information to new policy contexts that have little or no data of their own.¹⁶,¹⁷

## Processes of Ecosystem Services Valuation

Liu et al. (2010) argue that Ecosystem Services Valuation should not be used merely as a supplementary measure in the assessment process, but rather as a general practice that accounts for human impact on the ecosystem.¹⁶ This implies a broader consideration of ecosystem benefits than existing assessments typically capture, and, in the long term, could be considered an effective, globally applicable framework for estimating ecosystem services.

Costanza et al. (1997), in their report, attempted to assign values to the world's total ecosystem services using the following process:

- Identification of ecosystem services — a total of 17 ecosystem service categories were identified
- Identification of the biomes providing these services — 16 biomes were identified
- Estimation of unit area values for ecosystem services from each biome, per hectare per year (US$ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹)
- Summation of the total values across all biomes¹,¹⁸

### Incorporation into Existing Environmental Policies

Because ecosystem services are considered public goods, they are not fully utilized or quantified. Public goods generate positive or negative spillovers that private markets do not capture in their prices. Valuing ecosystems gives policymakers a quantitative basis for assessing benefits relative to the cost of implementing a policy — evaluation of ecosystems is thus seen as a means of improving information for better decision-making.¹⁹

Costanza et al. (1997) argue that GNP calculations would be improved by incorporating the value of ecosystem services, achievable by modifying national accounting systems to reflect this value. Several initiatives have since aimed to integrate Ecosystem Services Valuation as an instrument in environmental decision-making.²⁰,²¹,²²,²³,²⁴

Initiatives such as the EU 2020 Biodiversity Strategy, endorsed in May 2011, aim to determine the condition and value of ecosystem services, with findings to be incorporated into national accounting and reporting systems by 2020. Research conducted by The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity (TEEB) reflects considerable variation and a broad range of approaches to estimating ecosystem services, though results remain inconclusive. TEEB suggests that further studies, and more data from individual Member States, are needed to better incorporate Ecosystem Services Valuation into decision-making.²⁵

## Criticism of Ecosystem Services Valuation

Ecosystem valuation can be a difficult and controversial task, and environmental experts are often criticized for attempting to monetize nature. However, practitioners and conservation groups responsible for protecting and managing natural resources are frequently faced with difficult trade-off decisions in resource allocation. Costanza et al.'s (1997) attempt to monetize ecosystem services sparked a global debate among environmental experts, with critics arguing that placing monetary value on ecosystem services risks limiting their perceived appeal and value. Spangenberg et al. (2010) argue that, while this approach may be favorable for preserving ecosystems and their services, economic valuation methods alone are insufficient to determine the objectives of conservation policy. They contend that no single method can value a full range of services simultaneously; several valuation methods rest on the same underlying economic assumptions, so variation in results often reflects the choice of valuation method rather than genuine differences in the object under study.²⁶

Laurans et al. (2013) identified several shortcomings in the use of Ecosystem Services Valuation:²⁷

- **Lack of accuracy in valuation:** There is a need to improve valuation techniques to yield sufficiently accurate results. Costanza et al. (1997) acknowledge that an accurate estimate of ecosystem service value may be difficult to obtain given inherent uncertainties, though they note their study remains useful in highlighting where further research is needed.¹
- **Valuation may be inadequate:** Some services assessed through Ecosystem Services Valuation may not represent the real issues at stake and may therefore be insufficient for decision-making. Monetary pricing may not fully capture what decision-makers need to know to address issues that are irreversible or carry significant social consequences.
- **Cost of valuation may limit its use:** Ecosystem service valuations are often conducted on-site, making resulting values difficult to transfer elsewhere. Questions have also been raised about the methodology Costanza used to calculate biome values and how transferable these are across different locations.¹,¹⁸
- **Decision-makers may lack sufficient training in economics:** Some decision-makers lack the economic training needed to fully understand and apply these valuation tools. Conversely, Jakub Kronenberg (2014) found that a study on the economic value of bird-provided ecosystem services failed in part because of over-reliance on economic framing, at the expense of other relevant factors such as the relationship between human well-being and the ecosystem. He suggested broadening the scope of Ecosystem Services Valuation to account for non-monetary benefits as well,²⁸ implying that economic knowledge alone is not sufficient for making sound judgments about ecosystem services.
- **Valuation may render political strategies vulnerable by increasing transparency:** Some decision-makers may be reluctant to have the outcomes of their decisions made visible. The efficiency of Ecosystem Services Valuation cannot be maximized unless the public is adequately informed about relevant environmental decisions.

Liu et al. (2010) argue that while Ecosystem Services Valuation aims to make policy objectives as clear as possible, some technical experts and decision-makers may see public debate on environmental issues as a constraint on their freedom to act — making Ecosystem Services Valuation a politically sensitive topic in some contexts. Others argue that maximizing clarity in valuation helps society make better decisions by ensuring sufficient information is available. Farber et al. (2002) support this view, suggesting that a small group of stakeholders can be convened to advise on the social value of environmental services and help build consensus around better environmental decisions.¹⁷

## Case Study: Offshore Wind Farm

For this case study, a hypothetical offshore wind farm located in Denmark — referred to as Lund Offshore Wind Farm — is considered. It is located 9km off the coast of Hanstholm, with an installed capacity of 300 MW and an estimated cost of approximately 800 million Danish Kroner. The wind farm spans 12.7 square miles, with 400 meters between turbines and 700 meters between rows. Its 300 MW capacity is sufficient to supply approximately 200,000 residential homes annually. The site has a load factor of 38% and produces approximately 1,200 GWh of electricity per year.²⁹ The site also features scenic coastal landscapes that serve as a tourist attraction.

In practice, the ecosystem services associated with the marine environment are identified using the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment framework, and the potential impacts of the wind farm's installation on these services are assessed. Scenic views are considered public goods, since they provide cultural services in the form of recreation and ecotourism through their aesthetic value. Wind turbines can affect scenic views by their visual nature, and as a result, wind farm proposals are often influenced by turbine aesthetics.³⁰

The impacts of the offshore wind farm can be assessed by surveying tourists to examine the implications of turbine visibility for the local tourism industry. Relevant valuation methods here include Travel Cost, Hedonic Pricing, and Contingent Valuation. The Contingent Valuation method involves presenting respondents with an alternative scenario and asking either for their willingness to pay (WTP) to obtain or avoid the change, or their willingness to accept (WTA) compensation for the impact caused by the turbines' installation.²⁶

By assessing the impacts of wind farm operations on the marine environment within the framework of Ecosystem Services Valuation, we gain explicit insight into the trade-offs between human activity and ecosystem services. The long-term effects of human activity — in this case, the installation of wind turbines — can therefore be explored systematically within this framework, contributing to more accurate, better-informed environmental decision-making.³¹

It should be noted, however, that this case study focuses on a limited number of services and does not assess the ecosystem in its entirety. Another limitation is the lack of a reliable means of comparing these intangible goods to the economic values the ecosystem otherwise produces.³²

---

## Conclusion

It has been argued that valuation of ecosystem services provides useful statistics that can convey information about ecosystem value to policymakers. Yet some ecological experts consider this method unrealistic and ambiguous.¹⁹ Studies also show that available data are often insufficient to establish a fully comprehensive set of values reflecting the ecosystem. Nevertheless, it is generally believed that, even with imperfect data, Ecosystem Services Valuation can be constructed in ways that meaningfully assist decision-making. In light of these limitations, further research should be conducted on the benefits and challenges of ecosystem services valuation as a decision-support tool. Additionally, it is recommended that models be developed to capture ecological functions more thoroughly and consistently, in order to improve the overall decision-making process.

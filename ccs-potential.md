# The Potential of Carbon Capture and Storage: A Technology and Risk Assessment

---

## Abstract

Energy generation strongly depends on the burning of fossil fuels, which significantly contributes to global emissions of greenhouse gases. Although renewables have a role in the energy mix, combustion of fossil fuels is expected to increase in the future, as world energy demand continues to grow and must be met, in part, by fossil fuel generation. Global warming and climate change have prompted efforts to reduce carbon dioxide levels in the atmosphere, and Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) is considered one option for reducing CO₂ emissions.

A report reveals Norway's plans to achieve full carbon capture and storage by 2022, proposing to capture CO₂ from industrial plants, transport it by ship, and inject it into empty North Sea oil and gas reservoirs.⁷ This technique, however, carries some operational risks. This report reviews CCS technology — including CO₂ capture, transport, and storage — identifies these risks, suggests recommendations to make CCS a viable option, and concludes with opportunities for future improvement.

---

## Introduction

It is widely known that the earth is gradually warming, and that greenhouse gas emissions from burning fossil fuels contribute significantly to this warming. Fossil fuel power plants, vehicle exhausts, and industrial waste together contribute about 35.9 billion tons of CO₂ and other greenhouse gases into the atmosphere annually, accounting for roughly 70% of GHG emissions from these sectors.¹,²

This high volume of greenhouse gas emissions results from the high proportion of fossil fuel energy consumption across various sectors. Given this, CO₂ reduction technology is encouraged by policymakers and environmental regulators to reduce greenhouse gas emissions in power generation, transport, and other energy-intensive industries. The United States Census Bureau estimates that world population was expected to reach 7.6 billion by 2020,³ implying that global demand for fossil fuels will likely continue to increase — especially in developing countries, where a significant proportion of the population still lacks access to electricity.

The global agreement reached in December 2015 at the 21st UN Conference of the Parties (COP21) commits the world to reducing greenhouse gas emissions, with a goal of keeping the rise in global temperature below 2°C compared to pre-industrial levels. To achieve this, CO₂ emissions must be reduced at significant scale. The IEA reports that carbon capture and storage could contribute up to 14% of total energy-related CO₂ reductions by 2050.⁴

Various countries have adopted different approaches to reducing CO₂ emissions,⁵,¹⁹ including:

- Improving energy efficiency and promoting energy conservation
- Increasing the use of lower-carbon fuels, including natural gas, hydrogen, or nuclear power
- Switching to less carbon-intensive fuels — for example, gas instead of coal
- Using renewable energy sources such as wind, solar, bioenergy, and hydropower
- Capturing and storing CO₂

Following the Paris Agreement, the central global challenge is providing energy with low CO₂ emissions. Renewable energy resources play a major role in meeting this challenge but will eventually need support, since renewable resources are intermittent and currently limited in scale — renewable electricity generation contributes to only about 23.7% of total energy consumption.⁶ Moreover, given the large and inexpensive reserves of fossil fuels still available, they are likely to continue supporting electricity generation and industry in the coming years.

This century's energy outlook is likely to be a growing mix of renewables and traditional sources such as oil and gas, meaning some level of emissions will remain for the foreseeable future — which is why carbon capture and storage remains important. CCS captures CO₂ from factories and power plants and stores it safely underground, and is driven by the immediate need to address climate change. To make investments in clean energy technologies more attractive, governments must set an effective price on CO₂ emissions. The IEA has estimated that, by 2050, the cost of tackling climate change without CCS could be 70% higher than with it. CCS has the potential to reduce overall CO₂ reduction costs and increase flexibility in achieving greenhouse gas reductions.

This report reviews the prospects for CCS technology and the risks associated with its use, discusses the challenges to its adoption, and proposes possible solutions to make it a more attractive option for meeting the ambitions set out at the Paris Climate Summit.

---

## What Is Carbon Capture and Storage?⁸

Carbon capture and storage is a suite of technologies that can capture up to 90% of CO₂ emissions produced from the use of fossil fuels in electricity generation and industrial processes, preventing the release of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. The technology involves capturing CO₂ produced by large industrial plants, transporting it, and storing it permanently in stable rock formations underground at carefully selected, safe sites. According to the IEA's BLUE Map scenario, carbon capture and storage could account for up to 19% of global CO₂ emission reductions by 2050.⁹,¹⁰

Stored CO₂ can also be used in processes such as enhanced oil recovery or within the chemical industry — a process sometimes referred to as carbon capture and utilization (CCU).¹¹,¹² The capture process consists of three main stages: CO₂ capture, transport, and storage and monitoring.

### Application of CCS

In principle, CO₂ can be captured from various sectors that emit significant amounts of it, including electricity generation (industrial district heating and combined heat and power), industrial processes, and fuel processing. CO₂ emissions from sectors such as transport, agriculture, and residential use are more difficult to capture, since they are spread across a wide area; these can instead be addressed through alternative measures such as enhanced energy efficiency, renewables, and Combined Heat and Power paired with CO₂ capture technology. About 29% of global CO₂ emissions come from power generation, offering significant potential for capture from fossil fuel power plants. Lower concentrations of CO₂ can also be captured during the production of iron and steel, cement, chemicals, and pulp, and from oil refining and the production of synthetic fuels such as hydrogen and liquid transportation fuels.¹⁶

### Overview of CO₂ Capture Technology

CO₂ is formed during combustion, and the type of combustion process determines the choice of CO₂ removal method.⁵ CO₂ capture technologies fall into three categories: post-combustion capture, pre-combustion capture, and oxy-fuel combustion.

**Post-combustion capture:** CO₂ is captured from flue gas after combustion. This technology is generally preferred for retrofitting existing power plants and works efficiently at small scale, with CO₂ recovery of around 800 tonnes/day. It is mainly applicable to fossil fuel plants, where fuel is burned in the presence of air to produce steam that powers a turbine or generator. A major challenge for post-combustion capture is the large parasitic load it imposes on a power plant: low concentrations of CO₂ in the flue gas increase the cost of capturing high-purity CO₂ (above 95%), which can raise the cost of electricity production by as much as 70%.¹³,¹⁴

**Pre-combustion capture:** Coal or natural gas is pre-treated before combustion, and carbon is captured from the resulting syngas before combustion is completed. This process is applied to integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) power plants, where heat is used in the presence of steam and a limited oxygen supply to convert solid fuel into hydrogen and CO. The syngas then reacts further with steam, producing more hydrogen while the CO is converted to CO₂.

**Oxy-fuel combustion:** This process is applicable to fossil-fueled power plants, cement production, and the iron and steel industry. Fuel is burned in the presence of oxygen rather than air, producing flue gas with a high CO₂ concentration and reduced nitrogen content. Although technically feasible — since it avoids the need for chemical solvents — a major drawback is the significant amount of oxygen required, along with the environmental impacts associated with its production via air-separation processes.¹⁵

### CO₂ Storage Options

Captured CO₂ is isolated using separation techniques such as absorption, adsorption, and membrane separation,¹⁵ then compressed and transported by vessel or pipeline. CO₂ can be stored in the ground, in the ocean, or as minerals underground. Geological storage — the primary option — involves injecting CO₂ into formations such as depleted oil and gas reservoirs, unmineable coal seams, and deep saline aquifers at depths between 800 and 1,000m, where impermeable layers such as mudstones, clays, and shales help trap the CO₂.¹⁵,¹⁶

Geological storage is considered one of the more viable options, particularly where the history of depleted oil and gas reserves is well documented and existing well-drilling and injection techniques can be applied. Deep saline aquifer formations have an estimated storage capacity of 1,000–10,000 Gt, and around 920 Gt of CO₂ could potentially be stored in depleted oil and gas fields. The ocean also offers substantial storage capacity, though this option remains untested at large scale and requires further study given uncertain environmental impacts. CO₂ storage may also be combined with enhanced oil recovery (EOR), enhanced coalbed methane recovery (ECBM), or enhanced gas recovery (EGR) — options that could generate returns offsetting capture and transportation costs.¹⁹

### Alternative Storage Options

CO₂ can also be stored in the ocean by converting it into dry ice. Because dry ice is denser than seawater, it sinks to the ocean floor, where heat gradually melts it into liquid form, which then dissolves into the surrounding water. A separate approach involves dissolving CO₂ in water and injecting the mixture into rock formations, where it reacts with the rock to form a mineral called calcite — effectively calcifying and permanently storing the CO₂. Volcanic rocks, known as basalts, are particularly well suited to this process due to their high content of calcium, magnesium, and iron, which react readily with CO₂. Pilot projects using this method are underway in Iceland, which has abundant basalt deposits.¹⁷,¹⁸

### CCS Projects

The IEA reports that approximately 100 CCS projects are currently underway worldwide. Storage has been successfully demonstrated in deep saline aquifers and depleted oil and gas fields at the Sleipner and Snøhvit projects in Norway, and at Weyburn-Midale in Canada — all of which have been operating for more than a decade.¹⁵,¹⁶

### Industrial Uses of CO₂

Several industries, such as food and beverage production, use CO₂ as a carbonating agent, preservative, and solvent for flavor extraction and decaffeination. It is also used in pharmaceutical applications, both as a respiratory stimulant and in drug synthesis.

CO₂ can also be used in Carbon Capture and Utilization (CCU) processes, considered more commercially attractive than storage alone since captured CO₂ is converted into valuable products such as chemicals and fuels that can be sold. This approach is relatively inexpensive and non-toxic, creating an opportunity to treat CO₂ as a renewable resource in its own right.¹⁵,¹⁹

The primary concern with CO₂ storage remains the potential for environmental damage resulting from leaks. Annual leakage rates of less than 1% have been reported,²⁰ though the method is still widely regarded as risky and has received a cautious or negative public response.

## Why Is Carbon Capture Storage Controversial?

One of the most significant barriers associated with CO₂ storage is public perception.⁹ New technologies such as CCS often face uncertainty or opposition, since CO₂ can pose health risks if it escapes into the environment. CCS is a sensitive issue that has sparked considerable controversy, particularly among environmentalists concerned about the risks of CCS and its implications for renewable energy investment. Greenpeace, for example, has strongly opposed CCS development, arguing that the technology is unproven, risky, and expensive, and that investment in CCS risks undermining other climate change mitigation options. They further argue that large-scale CCS deployment could pose health risks, damage ecosystems, and contaminate groundwater.²¹,²² An online survey of students in Dresden, Germany found that willingness to pay (WTP) for CCS technology was considerably lower than for renewable energy technology, suggesting a broadly neutral public attitude toward CCS.²³

There is therefore a need for more pilot studies to demonstrate the technology in practice, which could help alleviate public concern and build confidence in CCS. For public perception to shift favorably, two conditions likely need to be met: global climate change must be widely regarded as a serious problem, and the public must accept that significant emissions reductions are necessary to address it. To support the development of large-scale CCS application, it is important to understand the associated risks and communicate them clearly to the public from an early stage in any project.²¹

The shortcomings associated with implementing CCS can be grouped into environmental and socio-economic factors, outlined below.

### Environmental Consequences of CCS

CO₂ is not toxic, but high concentrations can be detrimental to human health, potentially causing suffocation due to lack of oxygen. For this reason, all storage options should be rigorously tested at scale. While accidents resulting from underground CO₂ discharge are highly unlikely, there have been cases of natural CO₂ emissions causing localized risk situations.

- While CCS reduces CO₂ emissions, it may increase nitrogen and sulfur oxide emissions. Studies show CCS technology can increase nitrogen and sulfur dioxide emissions by up to 40% relative to a plant without capture.
- There is potential for long-term CO₂ leaks from the storage reservoir. Monitoring such leaks can be difficult, particularly for offshore storage, and identifying leaks during transport can also be challenging. Reported CO₂ leaks at the Weyburn field in Canada, observed by local farmers, prompted further investigation, though it remains unclear whether that research was completed.
- Studies suggest that injecting large volumes of CO₂ directly into the ocean may alter seawater chemistry by reducing its pH, increasing ocean acidity and potentially causing harm to marine ecosystems. This storage technique is considered more controversial than geological storage methods.
- CO₂ storage may induce minor earth tremors, potentially increasing leak risk, and gases such as methane can escape during the injection process.
- Sudden leaks could, in rare cases, prove fatal — the IPCC notes that CO₂ concentrations above 10%, even in the presence of oxygen, can be lethal.
- Leaks and underground flow may contaminate groundwater supplies.

### Socio-Economic Factors

**Increase in coal consumption:** Because CCS requires additional electricity and heat to operate, it increases both operational costs and fuel consumption relative to a plant without a CCS unit. For example, a coal-fired power plant fitted with CO₂ capture may need to burn roughly 30% more coal to meet these additional energy requirements. The cost per tonne of CO₂ emission reduction is therefore higher than the cost per tonne of CO₂ captured and stored — a gap that could narrow as capture energy efficiency improves. Retrofitting older plants with CCS is particularly inefficient, since energy output can fall by 20–30% due to the energy needed to compress and store the captured CO₂, potentially driving demand for new coal plants and further fossil fuel investment rather than encouraging renewable energy adoption.²⁴

**Increase in energy prices:** Carbon storage is likely to significantly increase energy prices, since more ambitious CO₂ reduction targets require more effective — and more costly — abatement technologies, a cost that is ultimately reflected in energy prices. According to the director of the Callide Oxyfuel Carbon Storage project, the technology could double the wholesale cost of electricity, raising retail costs by around 30%. The US Energy Information Administration (Ash, 2015) estimates that CCS could cost around 40% more per kg of avoided CO₂ emissions than photovoltaic energy, 125% more than wind, and 260% more than geothermal.²⁵,²⁶ In China, the cost of CCS at the Huaneng GreenGen IGCC plant in Beijing is estimated at 170 RMB/tonne, increasing the cost of electricity generation by roughly 0.16 RMB/kWh.

**Lack of financial incentives:** Companies undertaking CCS projects must guarantee stable, long-term storage options, which may be poorly suited to private companies with limited incentive to invest in abatement technology.

**High cost associated with CCS:** CCS remains a high-risk investment with substantial upfront costs and limited financial return, which increases the likelihood that developers will seek subsidies rather than proceed independently. There is also limited incentive to drive CCS innovation, since the technology can be readily replicated by competitors — though costs are expected to decline over time through technological deployment and sustained RD&D. A stakeholder survey identified cost, lack of financial returns, and rising electricity prices as the primary challenges facing CCS.

**Other identified barriers to implementing CCS include:**

- Lack of a sustainable market mechanism or clear incentives to encourage companies to develop CCS technology⁵
- Absence of policies penalizing major CO₂ emitters
- Lack of a legal framework to regulate the transport and storage of CO₂, both inland and offshore
- Lack of established proof validating the long-term safety of ocean-based CO₂ storage

## Suggestions and Recommendations

With fossil fuel consumption continuing to grow, CCS has a vital role to play in mitigating climate change. To make CCS a genuinely viable option, decision-makers and industry must actively encourage its development. The following recommendations aim to address the barriers identified above.

### Integrity of CO₂ Storage Sites

CCS technologies must be designed to deliver consistent environmental benefits that exceed the cost of capital, energy, and operation. CO₂ should be stored in reliable sites that can be readily switched if backup capacity is required, and storage sites must be stable enough to withstand long-term storage — achievable through thorough site investigation prior to CO₂ deposition. Robust CO₂ monitoring systems should be developed to detect leakage, and a well-designed pipeline network should be developed to streamline transportation and reduce costs. Clusters of storage sites, known as hubs, could also be used to serve multiple CO₂ sources, reducing costs through shared infrastructure.²⁷

### Regulatory Framework

Government should provide a strong regulatory and policy framework to guarantee the stability of CCS projects. At minimum, a supportive regulatory system should include guidelines governing:²⁸

- Performance requirements for CO₂ capture
- Safety, operation, and access standards for CO₂ transport pipelines
- CCS design, site selection, permitting, operation, and closure
- Long-term monitoring, remediation, and financial responsibility for CCS sites
- Health, safety, and environmental liability
- Liability for CO₂ leakage, including CO₂ reduction credits or obligations

Taxes should be imposed on CO₂ emissions to make emitting more expensive than capturing it.

### Optimizing CO₂ Emissions

CO₂ emissions management can be optimized by using Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) to offset part of CCS costs and generate revenue — a process in which CO₂ is injected into an oil reservoir to increase well pressure and reduce oil viscosity, thereby increasing flow and production. Captured emissions can also be used to extract coal from otherwise uneconomic geological beds.¹⁹

### International Cooperation

Technology transfer from industrialized countries should be encouraged to fully realize CCS's potential in developing countries. If CCS technology remains largely confined to industrialized nations, its overall impact and role will be significantly undermined — underscoring the importance of international cooperation.

### RD&D

Further studies should be conducted to establish the long-term effects of CO₂ on the marine ecosystem before ocean-based storage can be fully implemented, and more pilot projects should be developed to validate the integrity of various storage options.

### Providing Sufficient Information to the Public

Finally, sufficient information should be made available to the public to increase acceptance of the technology. When information is clearly understood, the public is better positioned to form informed opinions of CCS and make well-informed decisions regarding its deployment.

---

## Conclusion

Tackling climate change requires significant reductions in CO₂ emissions, achievable either by reducing coal consumption or by capturing CO₂ through CCS. CCS faces several technical and socio-economic constraints that continue to hinder large-scale deployment — overall costs remain high and would need to fall substantially before widespread implementation becomes viable, and environmental groups have raised legitimate concerns about impacts on the environment and human health.

The most significant barrier CCS technology must overcome is public acceptance of storing CO₂ underground. Without sufficient proof that CO₂ can be stored safely long-term, the technology's benefits will remain difficult to realize at scale. Achieving CCS's full emissions mitigation potential will require a significant increase in RD&D investment, achieved through more large-scale storage pilot projects and sustained public cooperation. RD&D efforts should prioritize storage projects in locations with low population density to minimize social barriers, and processes such as stakeholder consultation and review should be incorporated into all pilot projects, alongside robust monitoring strategies for storage sites. Finally, a comprehensive regulatory and legal framework for CO₂ storage projects must be established to address leakage, licensing, and landowner rights.

Countries should be given equal opportunity to incorporate CCS alongside other climate change mitigation technologies, supported by a mix of climate and economic policy instruments. The IEA notes that non-OECD countries have a major role to play in realizing global CO₂ mitigation goals, underscoring the need for outreach programs to developing countries and continued technology transfer from industrialized nations to fully realize CCS's potential. Establishing a global emissions trading scheme, a meaningful price on CO₂, and predictable returns on investment will all be necessary to stimulate the timely, large-scale implementation of CCS.

At the same time, the continuing decline in renewable energy costs has increased the viability of renewable generation and encouraged a broader shift toward low-carbon technologies. If current cost trends continue, renewables are likely to become increasingly cost-effective, remaining an attractive means of generating zero-emissions electricity. Renewables, nuclear, and CCS technologies can ultimately co-exist as part of a diversified, cost-effective portfolio for reducing CO₂ emissions from power generation — though the relative role of each will vary by region, depending on factors such as cost, policy, public acceptance, and the prevailing legal framework.

Looking forward, a gap remains between the current trajectory of CCS implementation and the path required for CCS to achieve its proposed 14% share of global CO₂ emission reductions by 2050. Closing this gap will require accelerated project development, demonstration, and implementation — supported, in turn, by stronger collaboration among CCS policymakers worldwide.

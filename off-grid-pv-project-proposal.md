## 1. Project Proposal

### Abstract

Nigeria is a developing country with a growing population of 174 million people. A majority of its populace, particularly in rural areas, live without access to electricity. This is because these areas are either geographically isolated or sparsely populated, and therefore located far from the national grid. This poses a serious challenge in supplying sufficient energy to the country's large population.

Solar photovoltaic technology can be considered one of the most reliable renewable energy technologies. This is of significant importance for developing countries like Nigeria, especially in rural dwellings where there is little or no access to electricity. The deployment of solar energy is expected to contribute to improving living conditions among rural dwellers, as well as reducing their dependency on fossil fuels.

### Background

This project proposal examines the installation of off-grid PV systems to meet energy demand in rural communities in Northern Nigeria. The high solar radiation in this region suggests that installing PV systems here will yield an optimum energy supply.

For the proposed project, Maiduguri has been selected for preliminary research. The monthly average insolation incident on a horizontal surface in Maiduguri is about 6.75 kWh/m² per day.

Maiduguri lies approximately at 13°05'E longitude and 11°51'N latitude, with an average elevation of 354 meters above sea level.¹ The two climatic seasons are the dry season (November–March) and the wet season (April–October). Maiduguri has good availability of solar radiation, with 23 MJ/m² as the annual average of daily global irradiation measured on the horizontal surface in the state. Monthly, the average varies from 17 MJ/m² as the minimum to 25 MJ/m² as the maximum.² The mean annual rainfall is 800mm, with a mean temperature of 40°C.² Average daily sunshine duration in Maiduguri is approximately 7.0 hours per day.

Maiduguri has an estimated population of 1.1 million and is located in the northeastern region of Nigeria, where sunshine reaches a maximum of about 7.0 kWh/m² per day. This implies that installing off-grid PV systems as a means of harnessing solar energy will reduce the region's dependency on fossil fuel. Additionally, the deployment of off-grid PV systems can be considered an important step in improving the socio-economic conditions of rural areas, and thus overall quality of living standards. As in other rural areas in the northeastern region, agriculture is the predominant activity, and household energy demand is mainly for cooking, agricultural, and small-scale industrial activities.³ Integrating PV systems into rural settlements offers various socio-economic benefits, such as job creation and public health. This region offers a genuine market for off-grid PV systems.

Nigeria's carbon emissions in 2008 were estimated at 90 million metric tonnes, with a slight decrease by 2012 (86 million metric tonnes).⁴ This project also seeks to reduce carbon emissions by providing electricity to meet energy demand in rural communities in Maiduguri.

### Project Objectives

The research intends to achieve the following objectives:

- Evaluate the efficiency of off-grid solar PV systems in generating electricity to meet energy demand in rural areas and other areas in Nigeria that cannot be connected to the grid in the near term
- Propose design principles for the components of an off-grid PV system
- Examine the carbon savings potential of off-grid solar PV
- Demonstrate the advantages of electricity generation by solar PV over electricity generated by conventional fossil fuel

### Resources

The resources used in this project are:

- Photovoltaic panels
- Inverter (90% efficiency)
- Tracking system
- Charge controller
- Batteries (allowable depth-of-discharge down to 20% capacity, with at least 80% round-trip efficiency)

### Beneficiaries of the Work

The project addresses the use of solar PV for the electrification of rural areas and reflects the analysis and findings of the author's independent research.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Introduction

Solar energy is one of the most abundant and largest potential energy sources in the world. Solar PV technology is among the safest and most reliable means of harnessing solar energy. Because it does not emit greenhouse gases, the application of solar PV as a substitute for fossil fuel is considered environmentally sustainable. Rapid technological development and declining costs have revolutionized the industry, further underpinning its huge potential for providing sustainable solutions to energy demand. Solar panels today are modest in size and sturdier — PV systems that were completely unaffordable two or three years ago are now considered among the most cost-effective ways to generate electricity for most applications, compared with other renewable energy technologies.

Most installed PV systems are in Europe, where total solar plant production exceeds 70 GW.⁵ However, developing countries, despite an abundance of sunlight almost year-round, remain behind in solar PV adoption. The World Bank⁶ observes that solar PV is the only technology that can function anywhere, notwithstanding geographic variation in the resource (the sun, in this case). For this reason, off-grid PV systems are considered a viable option for electricity supply in remote areas. The choice of this renewable resource is mainly because solar energy is not only an alternative energy option, but also has a positive environmental and economic impact in rural areas. Adding solar PV to the energy mix creates employment opportunities and boosts industry progress, creating opportunities for new markets to emerge.

### 2.2 Development of Solar Energy Technologies

Alexandre-Edmond Becquerel discovered that sunlight could be converted directly into electricity through his observation of the photovoltaic effect in 1839. Following this, in 1876, Adams and Day observed that selenium has photovoltaic properties. The first solar cell, developed by Chapin, Fuller, and Pearson, followed in 1954, with an efficiency of 6%.⁷,⁸,⁹,¹⁰

The conversion efficiency of silicon cells developed in 1958 was 11%, although the cost was very high at $1,000 per watt of power.¹⁸

The first practical adoption of solar technology was in space, on the orbiting satellite Vanguard 1 in 1958, at a time when there was no cost limitation since no other energy source was available. Further study led to the discovery of other photovoltaic materials, such as gallium arsenide (GaAs). At elevated temperatures, GaAs has higher performance than silicon but is much more expensive. According to the International Energy Agency's Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme (IEA-PVPS), the global installed capacity of PV at the end of 2014 was 177 GW, reflecting the rapid growth and widespread adoption of solar PV systems.¹¹

Photovoltaic cells are made of various semiconductor materials that conduct electricity. The most commonly used materials are silicon (Si), cadmium sulfide compounds (CdS), cuprous sulfide (Cu₂S), and gallium arsenide (GaAs). Photovoltaic cells are assembled into modules of approximately 36 cells.⁷

### 2.3 Generations of Solar Cells

Solar technologies currently in use are classified into three generations. **First-generation systems** use mono-crystalline and multi-crystalline silicon materials. These cells presently dominate the commercial market but are expensive to produce and have relatively low efficiency.

**Second-generation systems** are based on thin-film PV technologies: amorphous silicon (a-Si), micromorph silicon (a-Si/μc-Si), Cadmium-Telluride (CdTe), Copper-Indium-Selenide (CIS), and Copper-Indium-Gallium-Diselenide (CIGS). Amorphous silicon cells are made up of silicon atoms arranged in a uniform layer; due to their thin structure, they absorb light more effectively than crystalline and multi-crystalline silicon. These cells are cheap to produce but have lower efficiency, around 6%. Their low cost makes them suitable for applications that do not require high efficiency.¹²,¹³ Because silicon is abundant and environmentally safe, amorphous silicon is ideally suited for thin-film solar cells; it absorbs sunlight very well, so only a thin layer (~1μm) is required, compared to ~100μm for crystalline and multi-crystalline cells.¹⁴

**Third-generation systems** are based on organic, inorganic, and hybrid thin-film materials. These PV cells have high efficiency and low manufacturing cost; however, these technologies are not yet widely commercialized.¹⁵

### 2.4 Principles of Solar Energy Conversion

When two layers of semiconducting materials are brought close together and hit by light, an electrical current is produced — a process known as the photovoltaic effect. Electrons must be depleted on one layer so that, when exposed to sunlight, the layers absorb photons. The energy of the absorbed light then excites electrons, causing some to flow freely from one layer to another, generating current.

Silicon, the semiconducting material used to build a solar cell, is cut into very thin layers.¹⁶ Impurities are then added to the semiconductor layers to create an inequality of electrons — a process known as doping. The layers are then aligned together to form a solar cell.¹⁷,¹⁸,¹⁹

When photons strike a solar cell, they may be reflected, pass straight through, or be absorbed by the semiconducting material. A direct current is generated when electrons flow through the semiconductor. This current is then transferred through wires to an inverter, where it is converted to alternating current (AC), which powers electrical appliances. The greater the quantity of light absorbed by the solar cell, the greater the current generated.

Most electricity generated by solar cells comes from direct sunlight, although cells also generate electricity on overcast days. Individual solar cells typically produce only minute amounts of electrical energy, so cells are connected in series or parallel to generate useful amounts of electricity, forming photovoltaic modules. These modules can be grouped into solar arrays.¹⁶ (See Fig. 2)

A solar panel consists of a group of modules connected mechanically and electrically. A photovoltaic module is a group of cells connected electrically and assembled into a solar panel, which can then be merged into larger solar arrays.

### 2.5 Solar PV Technology Resources

There are two types of PV systems: grid-connected systems, which are connected to the utility grid, and off-grid systems, which supply power directly to a facility for immediate use. Grid-connected systems are typically used for residential and commercial facilities. Both types are based on first-generation technology.²⁰

#### 2.5.1 Grid-Tie PV System

In a grid-connected system, the PV system is connected to the local electricity network. Electricity generated during the day can either be used immediately (typical for commercial buildings) or sold to a utility company when not in use or in surplus. During hours when the solar PV system cannot generate electricity, power can be bought back from the electricity company. In effect, the grid acts as an energy storage system, so the PV system does not require battery storage.¹⁴

Grid-connected systems work especially well in hot, sunny climates, where peak electricity demand often coincides with maximum sunshine, due to high use of air conditioning systems.

#### 2.5.2 Off-Grid PV System

In contrast, a stand-alone (off-grid) PV system does not rely on the electricity grid, and the energy produced is normally stored in batteries. A typical stand-alone system consists of a PV module, battery, and charge controller. The system may also include an inverter, used to convert the direct current (DC) generated by the PV modules to the alternating current (AC) required by standard appliances.

Off-grid systems are used in areas without access to the electricity grid and where traditional infrastructure is unavailable. Off-grid PV systems are reliable and require relatively little maintenance, since their output depends only on variation in solar exposure. They generally require a battery for storage and to regulate power. Due to declining PV system costs, installations are now increasingly cost-effective — particularly beneficial for remote areas.²¹

#### 2.5.3 Hybrid PV System

Hybrid systems combine photovoltaic modules with a supplementary generation method, such as a diesel or gas generator. For optimum performance of both generation sources, hybrid systems are operated in a more sophisticated manner than stand-alone PV systems. A common problem with hybrid systems is inadequate control of the diesel generator: if the charge supplied by the generator to the batteries is too high, PV-generated energy is wasted; if batteries are insufficiently charged, their lifecycle may be shortened. This can be addressed by installing an automatic system to start the engine and regulate its output.²²

### 2.6 Components of an Off-Grid PV System

A photovoltaic system is made up of balance-of-system (BOS) components, consisting of a battery bank, charge controllers, and inverters, described below.⁸,¹⁴,¹⁸

*Fig 2: Stand-alone PV system with AC load¹⁸*

#### 2.6.1 Batteries

PV systems require battery storage of electricity to provide energy at night or on cloudy days. For optimum performance, storage batteries should be able to be discharged and recharged as many times as possible without damage.

#### 2.6.2 Charge Controllers

Charge controllers regulate the battery by monitoring the charge rate and ensuring the battery is sufficiently charged when current is needed. They also ensure the battery is not overcharged or excessively discharged, protecting it from damage.²³

#### 2.6.3 Inverters

Inverters convert the direct current (DC) from the battery to alternating current (AC) to power systems and applications that require it. Solar PV generates DC, which is stored in batteries; since most appliances use AC, an inverter is used to convert stored DC to usable AC.

#### 2.6.4 Diodes

Diodes are electrical devices used to stop the flow of current from the battery bank to the PV panels at night, protecting the modules. They are otherwise known as blocking diodes.

### 2.6.5 Barriers to the Deployment of Off-Grid PV Systems in Rural Areas

Solar PV has huge potential, and there are real environmental and socio-economic benefits associated with off-grid solar PV in rural areas — including job creation, access to information technology, improved standard of living, reduced dependency on fossil fuel, diversification and security of electricity supply, and increased rural electrification.²⁴,²⁵,²⁶ However, achieving successful dissemination of solar technology requires addressing several prevailing barriers:²⁷–³⁵

- Lack of awareness and information about the economic and environmental benefits of solar energy technologies
- Lack of sufficient knowledge and willingness to invest in new technologies
- Lack of sufficient funds and high initial capital cost of installation
- Lack of skilled personnel and adequate training facilities
- Lack of access to solar radiation data stations and identification of good geographical locations
- Lack of research and development centres and financial institutions to support solar energy technologies
- Social acceptance of solar energy technologies relative to the conventional electricity grid
- Lack of maintenance
- Insecurity of installation (theft and vandalism of PV components)
- Unavailability of land, since most rural dwellers use their land for agricultural purposes; installing PV systems on arable land could compromise productivity and cause tension among landowners

### 2.6.6 Suggestions and Recommendations

To overcome these barriers and enable effective, efficient deployment of solar electricity in rural areas, the following recommendations are proposed:

1. Provision of appropriate government policies to expand the dissemination and penetration of solar technologies, encouraging adoption through subsidies and incentives such as feed-in tariffs
2. Raising public awareness and knowledge of solar energy's environmental and socio-economic benefits
3. Implementing small-scale solar energy pilot projects across different regions to demonstrate feasibility and provide precise information
4. Improving the level of research and development (R&D) in solar technology through funding and expansion of research institutes and higher education institutions
5. Providing adequate and efficient training for personnel to maintain and repair solar components
6. Establishing a database to collect and analyze solar irradiation data on a regular basis, so that this information is readily accessible for large-scale solar power projects
7. Eliminating taxes on the importation of solar PV components to reduce initial investment cost, while discouraging the importation of inferior components that wear out quickly
8. Placing restrictions on the importation of diesel and fuel-engine generators, given their negative environmental effects

The procedures involved in the research are outlined below.

### 3.1 Solar Resource Assessment

PV modules must be installed at the correct tilt angle to achieve maximum efficiency. One equivalent sun hour corresponds to a solar irradiance of 1,000 W/m². This value is used to determine the performance of solar cells and modules. Using available solar irradiation data for a given location, the equivalent sun hours can be determined. For example, the average annual solar irradiation in the United Kingdom is approximately 1,000 kWh/m². Since solar irradiance is 1,000 W/m² (1 kW/m²), this can be expressed as 1,000 kWh/m² ÷ 1 kW/m² = 1,000 equivalent sun hours, i.e., 1,000h ÷ 365 days ≈ 2.8 h/day.

Solar irradiation data would be collected from relevant institutes with accurate records for the area under study.²² To obtain maximum efficiency from the solar PV system, panels should be tilted at the optimum angle. Recommended monthly tilt angles will be collected from secondary sources and previous studies conducted within the region.

### 3.2 Assessment of Energy Demand

The design of the PV system starts with determining the initial load and available solar energy per unit area. Daily energy demand data would be gathered using a range of methods, including surveys, direct observation, informal conversations, and informal interviews. Total daily electricity demand for an average household has been estimated at approximately 0.956 kWh.

### 3.3 Weather and Climate Considerations

The project is expected to operate effectively year-round and must therefore be sized for the months receiving the least sunlight in some parts of the country. In Nigeria, this occurs during the rainy season (December to March) and on overcast days. Areas in southern Nigeria receive as little as 5 hours of sunlight per day. The battery bank will therefore be sized to store enough energy for 6 days of autonomy, to allow for extended periods without sufficient sunshine — requiring four dry-cell batteries (200 Ah, 12V).

### 3.4 Battery Sizing

The proposed battery is expected to have a nominal voltage of 12V and must power the load for three days without recharging while remaining above 20% charge capacity, with a round-trip efficiency of 80%. The battery bank must be sized to supply energy to loads at night and on overcast days.

The storage capacity of the battery bank in ampere-hours (Ah) is based on the number of consecutive cloudy days, battery efficiency, depth of discharge, and inverter efficiency, and can be determined using:⁴¹,⁴²

**M_batt = (A_d × D_L) / (η_batt × η_inv × DoD × V_s)**

where *A_d* is the maximum number of continuous overcast days, *DoD* is the maximum depth of discharge, and *V_s* is the system voltage (typically 12, 24, 48… V).

The required number of batteries for the system can be determined using:

**n_batt = M_batt / M_sin**

where *M_sin* is the storage capacity of a single battery.

### 3.5 Sizing of PV Module

The research will use a mono-silicon photovoltaic panel with a maximum power point tracker control method, chosen for its relatively high efficiency of 20%.⁹ Based on estimated load demand, the required PV array area can be determined using:

**PV_area = D_L / (H_t(av) × η_PV × L_Tcf × η_batt × η_inv)**

where *PV_area* is the required PV array area (m²), *D_L* is daily energy demand (kWh/day), *H_t(av)* is average irradiation available per day (kWh/m²/day), *η_PV* is panel efficiency (%), *η_batt* is battery efficiency (%), *η_inv* is inverter efficiency (%), and *L_Tcf* is the temperature correction factor (typically −0.4 to −0.5% per K for crystalline silicon). Batteries and inverter are assumed to have efficiencies of 80% and 90% respectively.

The average global solar irradiation of the site on an optimally tilted surface is given by:

**H_t(av) = H / (1 − 4.46×10⁻⁴ × b_opt − 1.19×10⁻⁴ × b_opt²)**

where *b_opt* is the optimum tilt angle and *H* is the measured global solar radiation on a horizontal surface (Wh/m²).

The solar power required by the load can be calculated as:

**PV_peak_power = PV_area × H_STC × η_PV**

where *H_STC* is the peak solar radiation under standard test conditions (1,000 W/m²).

The number of modules needed can be estimated as:

**N_modules = PV_peak_power / S_peak_power**

where *S_peak_power* is the peak power of the selected PV module.

#### 3.5.1 Panel Inclination

To maximize panel output, the inclination would be adjusted monthly to match the sun's zenith angle. The zenith angle can be calculated from latitude, daily declination angle, and hour angle:

**Zenith angle: cos θz = cos Φ cos δ cos ω + sin Φ sin δ**

where negative angles correspond to southern latitudes and south-tilting panels; *Φ* is latitude and *ω* is the hour angle. The declination angle is calculated as:

**δ = 23.45 × sin(360 × (284 + n) / 365)**

where *n* is the day of the month, beginning with *n* = 1 on January 1st.⁴³ This is used to calculate average monthly inclination angles for Maiduguri.

### 3.6 Selection of Charge Controller

The charge controller safely charges the batteries, ensures they are not overcharged, and helps preserve battery lifecycle. The correct charging current for the PV system can be determined using:

**I_ch = PV_peak_power / V_s**

### 3.7 Inverter

The selected inverter must be able to operate the maximum expected AC load at any given hour of the day, and should therefore be rated at least 20% higher than the total rated power of the recommended AC loads. Inverter power can be calculated as:

**P_inv = P_ac / pf**

where *P_ac* (kW) is the maximum AC load.

### 3.8 Carbon Emission Savings

CO₂ savings can be determined as follows:

**E_CO2 (kg/yr) = S_E(CO2) (kg/litre) × F_sav (litre/yr)**

**E_CO (kg/yr) = S_E(CO) (kg/litre) × F_sav (litre/yr)**

Once CO₂ savings are known, carbon emission savings can be determined using:

**E_carbon = 0.27 × E_CO2 (kg/yr)**

where:

**F_sav (litre/yr) = F_sav × 8760**

Here, *S_E(CO2)* is the specific CO₂ emission per litre of fuel, *S_E(CO)* is the specific emission of carbon monoxide (CO) per litre of fuel, and *F_sav* is the fuel consumption saved in litres per hour (see Appendix 1).

## 4. Conclusion

With fossil fuels at risk of depletion in the near future, renewable sources are considered to have high energy potential. It has been observed that a wide range of renewable energy technologies will increasingly compete with conventional energy sources in the coming years. Solar photovoltaic technology is presently one of the fastest-growing industries worldwide, with broad research underway and significant performance development already achieved. The wide range of solar PV applications is attributable to increasing efficiency, lower cost, and minimal greenhouse gas emissions.

This paper first gave a general overview of solar PV technology and the principles of solar energy conversion. It then outlined barriers to the successful deployment of off-grid PV systems in rural areas, based on global objectives to improve energy efficiency, reduce fossil fuel dependency, and increase carbon-free energy sources. Finally, suggestions and recommendations to overcome these barriers were presented, which could optimize the deployment of solar technology, promoting the growth of a low-carbon economy and sustainable regional development. It is hoped that this study may provide useful background for policymakers and environmental institutions seeking to enhance sustainable development through the implementation of off-grid PV systems.
